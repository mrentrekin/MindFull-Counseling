Read this first

If there is violence, coercion or controlling behavior, stalking, threats, or thoughts of suicide, this course is not the right primary intervention and should not be used to keep a dangerous situation contained. Contact a qualified local professional or emergency services. In the U.S., the 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline (call or text 988) and the National Domestic Violence Hotline (1-800-799-7233, or text START to 88788) are available around the clock.

This is educational material. It is not therapy, not a diagnosis, not legal advice, and not a verdict on whether your relationship should continue.

What this course is trying to do

Discovery collapses time. Everything feels like it has to be decided tonight — whether to stay, what to tell people, whether anything that happened before was real. Almost none of it does.

The work of the next several months is not to produce a verdict. It is to slow the crisis down enough that both of you can think, to stop the injury from repeating, to build an honest account of what happened, and to gather enough evidence — real evidence, not promises — to make a decision you can live with either way.

This course does not assume you will stay together. It does not assume you will separate. It assumes that whichever you choose, you would rather choose it clearly than be swept into it.

How the modules work

Each module has an objective, four or five lessons, and a set of tools you fill in as you go. Every lesson follows the same three beats:

Understand

What is actually happening — in the relationship, in the body, in the research. The teaching part.

Reframe

The single shift in how you are seeing it. One sentence, meant to be argued with and then kept.

Act

The practice. Something written, said, scheduled, or stopped this week.

Work at the pace of the harder week, not the easier one. Most couples spend two to four weeks per module, and many spend longer in Module 1 than they expect to. Progress is not how fast you move; it is whether honesty, follow-through, and the ability to recover from a hard conversation are increasing.

Two people, two copies

Wherever there is a writing space, both partners answer it — separately, before comparing. Print or save your own copy so neither of you is composing an answer while reading the other's. What you write here stays in your browser on this device; nothing is transmitted anywhere.

Working agreements

Before the first module, agree on the conditions that make the rest of it possible. Check what you can both commit to for the next ninety days. Anything you cannot agree to yet is the first thing to bring to a therapist.

Ninety-Day Working Agreement

Tool 0.1

Four questions, all the way through

When a conversation starts to come apart — and they will — return to these. They are the spine of the entire course.

1

What is happening in me?

Name the emotion, the thought, the body sensation, and the urge, before acting on any of them.

2

What is my part?

Own your choices, reactions, avoidance, defensiveness, escalation. The betrayed partner's part is never the affair.

3

What does my partner need to understand?

Say the experience underneath the accusation, the defense, or the demand.

4

What would move us toward clarity?

Choose the next useful behavior: disclose, listen, pause, apologize, set a boundary, get help, or schedule the conversation for a time you can both hold.

The aim is not to erase what happened. It is to find out whether you can build a relationship in which truth, accountability, connection, and integrity are stronger than they were before the rupture.

1.1

Find what is still bleeding

Understand

An affair is one injury. What follows the affair is often a series of smaller injuries that keep the wound open: contact that hasn't actually ended, a second version of the story that contradicts the first, a receipt found three weeks later, a phone checked at 2 a.m., an argument that ends with someone saying the thing they can't take back.

Recovery research consistently finds that repeated discovery — learning the truth in installments — does more sustained damage than the original disclosure. Each new fragment resets the clock and teaches the betrayed partner's nervous system that reality is unstable and that more is coming. Before anything else can be worked on, the leaking has to stop.

Notice that most of this list is not about the affair. It is about what the two of you are doing now, in week two or week six, that is making the next month harder than it has to be.

Reframe

You are not trying to feel better this week. You are trying to stop making it worse.

1.2

Boundaries that hold

Understand

A boundary is not a feeling or a request for reassurance. It is a specific, observable rule about behavior, with a stated consequence, that the person setting it can actually enforce. "I need you to be honest with me" is a wish. "Any message from her gets shown to me the day it arrives, and if I find one you didn't show me, we're going back to a counselor before we talk about anything else" is a boundary.

Structural family work has long held that a couple in crisis needs its perimeter re-drawn before its interior can be repaired — the third person has to be outside the system, definitively, before the two people inside it can face each other. Where the affair partner is a coworker, a friend, or someone in a shared community, "ending contact" requires a plan with details: what happens at work, what happens if they appear at an event, what gets said, who gets told.

Write no-contact as an action, not a promise. If it requires changing a number, leaving a group, changing a shift, or telling a supervisor, name that here.

Reframe

A boundary the betrayed partner has to police is not yet a boundary. It is surveillance with extra steps.

Tool 1.2

On transparency with an end date

Open access to phones and accounts can steady the first months. It cannot be the permanent architecture of the relationship — a marriage held together by monitoring eventually runs on vigilance rather than trust. Give the arrangement a review date now, in Module 4, rather than discovering in a year that neither of you knows how to end it.

1.3

Flooding and the twenty-minute rule

Understand

In Gottman's observational research, partners in conflict frequently reach a state he called diffuse physiological arousal — heart rate climbing past roughly 100 beats per minute, stress hormones rising, peripheral vision narrowing. In that state, access to humor, memory, problem-solving, and empathy measurably drops. People flooded in this way are not choosing to be unreasonable; the equipment for reasonableness is temporarily offline.

Two things follow. First, nothing said in that state should be treated as the final word — not the confession, not the accusation, not "I'm done." Second, recovering takes real time. Twenty to thirty minutes of genuine distraction is the working figure; two minutes of standing in the kitchen rehearsing your rebuttal is not a break, it is a countdown.

Flooding shows up differently in each partner. One escalates, pursues, keeps talking, needs the answer now. The other goes flat, quiet, and unreachable — which reads as indifference and usually is not. Both are the same physiology going in opposite directions.

Reframe

A time-out is not abandonment when it comes with a return time. Without one, it is.

Act

If you flood by escalating

Your urge is to keep going until it's resolved. It won't resolve in this state; it will get said in a way you'll have to repair later.

Practice: say the exact words "I need twenty minutes, and I'll come back at 8:40," and then leave the room without the parting shot.

If you flood by shutting down

Your system is protecting you by going still. To your partner it looks like you don't care, which raises the volume, which shuts you down further.

Practice: before you go quiet, say "I'm going offline, not walking away — give me twenty minutes."

Trigger and Regulation Plan

Tool 1.3

A physiological reset that works in ninety seconds

Two inhales through the nose — a full one, then a second short one on top of it — followed by a long, slow exhale through the mouth. Repeat five or six times. This pattern shifts the balance toward the parasympathetic side faster than trying to think your way calm, and it can be done standing in a hallway without anyone noticing.

1.4

Keep the machine running

Understand

Betrayal reliably disrupts sleep, appetite, concentration, and the sense that the world is predictable. Intrusive images, hypervigilance, and sudden waves of grief or rage are common responses to a relational injury of this size — not evidence that someone is weak or "not over it."

The practical problem is that every skill this course asks for — regulation, listening, honest answering, judgment about your own future — degrades sharply when you are not sleeping and not eating. Couples routinely try to do the hardest emotional work of their lives at 1 a.m. on four hours of sleep, and then conclude from how badly it went that the relationship is unsalvageable.

Guard the basics for the next month the way you would guard them for someone recovering from surgery. Also guard the perimeter: work performance, parenting, and your own health are what you will still need whichever way this goes.

Reframe

Nothing important gets decided after 10 p.m. Write it down, and take it to a time when you're both awake.

When to bring in a professional now, not later

Sleep that has not returned after two weeks, panic that interrupts work or driving, drinking that has increased since discovery, an inability to eat, thoughts of harming yourself, or a child showing distress at school — these are reasons to get individual support alongside this material, not signs that you are handling it badly.

1.5

Who else knows, and who needs to

Understand

A couple sits inside layers of other systems — children, extended family, friendships, a workplace, sometimes a congregation. Each of those layers responds to the news in ways that will still be in effect long after the two of you have moved on, which is why disclosure decisions made in the first week are so often regretted.

Three questions clarify most of them. Does this person need the information to stay safe or make an informed decision? Can this person hold it without becoming an actor in our marriage? And can I un-tell them later? The answer to the third is always no.

Children are the sharpest case. They almost always know something is wrong and are usually served by an honest, developmentally appropriate acknowledgment — "we're having a hard time, we're working on it, it isn't your fault, and both of us still take care of you" — and almost never served by the details, by being asked to choose, or by carrying messages. Age, what they have already overheard, and whether the family structure is changing all shift what to say.

Reframe

Every person you tell becomes part of the decision. Choose them the way you'd choose a jury.

2.1

Responsibility comes before explanation

Understand

There is a sequence here that cannot be reversed without damage. The partner who had the affair takes full ownership of the choices and the consequences first. Only then does the question of how it became possible get opened. Reversing the order — leading with loneliness, with the marriage, with what wasn't happening at home — converts an explanation into a bill, and the betrayed partner hears, correctly, that they are being charged for their own injury.

Terry Real's relational work puts it bluntly: the person who did the harm holds the harm, whole, without a co-signer. Unhappiness in a marriage explains a decision to complain, to demand change, to seek therapy, to leave. It does not explain a decision to deceive. That step was taken alone.

Full ownership has a recognizable shape. It names the specific behavior rather than "what happened." It doesn't hedge with "if I hurt you." It doesn't include the word but. It states the impact from the other person's side. And it doesn't ask, in the same breath, for anything back — not absolution, not credit, not an end to the subject.

Reframe

Context explains vulnerability. It never transfers responsibility. Both partners have to be able to hold that sentence for this module to work.

2.2

The relationship before the affair

Understand

Most marriages that end up here were not in open warfare. Far more often they were quietly drifting: two people managing logistics, avoiding a few subjects, each waiting for the other to reach first. Gottman's term for the slow tilt is negative sentiment override — the point at which neutral behavior starts being read as evidence of not caring, and repair attempts stop landing.

Underneath it there is usually a cycle. One partner presses for contact — asks, criticizes, escalates — because the silence feels like distance. The other withdraws, placates, or goes flat because the pressing feels like failing. Each move makes the other's more likely. Emotionally focused therapy calls this the negative cycle and treats it, not either partner, as the problem in the room. Systemic therapists make the same point about circularity: in a loop, there is no first move to find.

Two cautions. Mapping the cycle is not the same as splitting the blame — the cycle explains distance, not deception. And this is not the betrayed partner's chance to be told what they did wrong. It is a joint description of a pattern that had both of you in it, produced with far more care about how it lands than a normal argument would require.

Reframe

The cycle is the problem. Neither of you is the problem — and the affair is still one person's choice.

Act

When I feel the distance, I tend to

Press, ask again, criticize, check, explain at length, bring it up at the worst time, get louder, get sharper.

When I feel the pressure, I tend to

Go quiet, agree to end it, work later, scroll, promise, leave the room, say nothing and decide something.

2.3

What each of you brought with you

Understand

People arrive in a marriage carrying instructions written long before it. Bowen's central idea is differentiation — the capacity to stay yourself while staying connected, to tolerate someone else's disappointment without either collapsing into it or cutting off from it. Under stress, low differentiation shows up as fusion, distance, or a third party pulled in to absorb the pressure. An affair is one of the most common third points a couple recruits.

Satir described the postures people take when their sense of self is threatened: placating, blaming, going hyper-rational, or distracting. Each is a survival move learned somewhere. Most of us can name our own in about ten seconds and our partner's even faster.

This lesson asks the involved partner for the harder inventory — entitlement, secrecy as a habit, validation-seeking, conflict avoidance, impulsivity, an old belief that needs are shameful. It also asks the betrayed partner to look at their own patterns, but for a strictly different reason: not to explain the affair, but because the same patterns will shape how the next several months go, and because you are entitled to your own work regardless of what happens to the marriage.

Reframe

Doing your own work is not conceding a point. It is refusing to leave your future in the hands of what happened to you.

2.4

Guilt builds, shame collapses

Understand

Guilt says I did something harmful. Shame says I am something harmful. The first keeps a person facing outward, toward the damage and the repair. The second turns them inward, and what comes out of that turn is nearly always one of three things: defensiveness, collapse, or disappearance.

This matters practically, because a shame-flooded partner cannot listen. Halfway through hearing what they cost someone, they slide into self-attack, and the conversation reorganizes itself around comforting them. The betrayed partner then discovers they cannot describe their own injury without having to manage the other person's despair — and stops describing it.

Shame also has a mirror on the other side. Betrayed partners frequently report feeling foolish for not knowing, ashamed of still wanting the relationship, ashamed of the rage, ashamed of checking a phone at 3 a.m. None of that is a character flaw. It is what happens when the person who was your safest place became the source of the injury.

Reframe

"I'm a terrible person" ends the conversation. "Here is what I did to you, and I'm not going anywhere" continues it.

For the betrayed partner

You are not responsible for managing your partner's shame, and you should not have to soften an account of your own injury to keep them upright. If every attempt to describe what this did to you ends with you consoling them, name that as a pattern and take it to a therapist — it will not resolve on its own.

2.5

A story you can both live with

Understand

Betrayal breaks the story. The account you had of your marriage — who your partner was, what the last two years were, whether the anniversary trip was real — no longer holds, and until something coherent replaces it, the mind keeps going back to reconstruct it. That is what the 3 a.m. questions are: not curiosity, but an attempt to make reality stable again.

Narrative therapists work by separating the problem from the person and then thickening the account — filling in what else was true, what was resisted, what the person's actual values were, so the story has more in it than the worst chapter. A shared narrative after an affair needs four things: what happened, what made it possible, what it damaged, and what has to change. Sufficient truth, not a forensic transcript.

There is a real difference between the details that restore reality and the details that only supply images. Duration, whether it was one person or several, whether there was risk to your health, whether money was spent, whether people you know were involved, whether it was over before you found out — these bear on decisions you have a right to make. Choreography does not, and once described, it does not leave.

Reframe

You are not owed every image. You are owed a version of reality you can stand on.

Act

Shared Narrative Worksheet

A working rule for questions

Before asking, sort it: does the answer change a decision I have to make, or does it give me a picture I will carry? Ask the first kind directly and expect a full answer. Hold the second kind for Module 3, where you'll build a three-list system for handling them rather than swallowing or firing them at 1 a.m.

Understanding is not absolution. It is the difference between "I don't know why he did it" and knowing exactly what would have to change for it never to happen again.

3.1

Build the container

Understand

Couples in the months after discovery tend to talk about the affair in one of two ways: constantly and without shape, or not at all until it erupts. Both leave the same residue — the sense that the subject is bottomless and that any evening can be lost to it.

A container fixes what an open-ended conversation cannot. It has a stated purpose, a start and a stop, one topic, and an agreed way to end early. Twenty minutes is long enough to say something real and short enough that both partners can stay regulated inside it. Ending on time, especially when it is going badly, is itself the practice: it teaches both nervous systems that this subject has edges.

Schedule these. Ambushes — at the door, in the car with children present, at midnight — reliably produce the worst version of both of you. If something surfaces between scheduled talks, write it down and bring it to the next one; the writing itself does a surprising amount of the work.

Reframe

Not every question has to be answered tonight. A subject with a scheduled time is not a subject being avoided.

Act

3.2

Say the thing underneath

Understand

Gottman's finding on start-up is one of the sturdiest in couples research: how a difficult conversation begins predicts how it ends, and it predicts it within the first few minutes. A conversation that opens with contempt or a global accusation rarely recovers, however reasonable the middle is.

Underneath most accusations is something more accurate and much more exposed. Under "you don't even care what you did to me" is usually I'm terrified this is who you are. Under "you're still interrogating me" is usually I don't know how to be forgiven and I'm afraid this is permanent. Satir called the honest, non-defensive posture leveling: standing in what is actually true for you without attacking or shrinking.

This is not a request to be nice. Anger is legitimate here and does not need to be laundered. The request is to be accurate — to lead with the injury rather than the verdict, because the verdict is the part your partner will defend against and the injury is the part they can actually respond to.

Reframe

Lead with the wound, not the case for the prosecution. The case gets defended; the wound gets answered.

3.3

Validation is not agreement

Understand

The most common listening failure after an affair is defending the facts. The betrayed partner says "you were gone every Thursday for months," the involved partner says "it was four times," and the conversation dies on a technicality while the actual message — I have been re-reading my own life and finding holes in it — goes unanswered.

Emotionally focused work treats the listener's job as reaching for the emotion under the content and reflecting it accurately enough that the speaker feels met. You are not conceding a fact by saying "you looked at the calendar and every Thursday turned into a question." You are confirming that their experience registered.

Accuracy matters more than warmth here. "I hear you" without content reads as management. Reflect the specific thing, name the feeling you think is under it, and then ask whether you got it right — and be willing to be told you didn't.

Reframe

Correcting the facts before acknowledging the feeling is how a defensible position loses a marriage.

Act

The listener's four moves

Reflect the specific thing they said. Name the feeling you think is under it. Ask if you got it right. Only then, if it matters, offer your correction — as information, not as a defense.

What kills it

"That's not what happened." "You always exaggerate." "I've apologized a hundred times." Silence with a flat face. Apologizing so fast that nothing has to be heard.

3.4

Questions, and the three lists

Understand

Questions after betrayal are not one thing. Some restore reality and support decisions the betrayed partner is entitled to make. Some are attempts to find the moment where it could have been prevented — a search that never ends because that moment does not exist. Some are self-injury: requests for images that will be replayed for years.

A three-list system gives the questions somewhere to go. Ask now: the ones that bear on health, safety, money, exposure, whether it is truly over, and who else knows. Ask later: the ones about meaning — what he thought about, whether she compared, what it says about the marriage — which land better once the account in Module 2 exists. Don't ask: the ones you already know will lodge in your mind permanently.

The involved partner's obligation is to answer the first list completely and without editing, including the parts that make them look worse, and to keep answering later questions without sighing about having answered before. Repetition is not always interrogation. Sometimes it is a nervous system checking whether the answer is still the same — and it will keep checking until the answer has been consistent for a long time.

Reframe

Ask what you need to know to make decisions. Be careful about what you need to know to torture yourself with.

Act

The Three Lists

Tool 3.4

For the partner answering

Answer the whole question, once, in full. Do not release information in the smallest survivable increments — that is the pattern that made the first months unbearable. If you genuinely do not know or do not remember, say that plainly instead of inventing something plausible; a discovered invention costs more than the original gap.

3.5

Repair after it goes wrong

Understand

Some conversations will go badly. The measure that matters is not whether you escalate but how long it takes to come back and what condition the relationship is in when you do. Gottman's work on repair attempts found that the couples who last are not the ones who avoid rupture; they are the ones whose repair attempts get received — a joke, a hand, "I'm sorry, that was a low shot, can we start over."

A repair after an affair has an additional demand. The involved partner has to be able to hear "this is because of what you did" without treating each mention as a fresh attack, for considerably longer than feels fair. The betrayed partner has to be able to distinguish a bad hour from a verdict, and to say what happened in the escalation that was theirs.

The most useful sentence either of you can learn is the one that names the pattern instead of the person: "We're in it again." It is the same move as calling the cycle the problem, deployed live, at the moment it is happening.

Reframe

Recovery is not the absence of bad nights. It is bad nights that get shorter, and mornings that come back.

4.1

Trust is a behavior, not a feeling

Understand

Trust is not restored by declaration. It is rebuilt out of a long series of small moments in which words and behavior match — Gottman's "sliding door" moments, the unremarkable instants where one partner reaches and the other either turns toward them or doesn't. Nobody remembers the individual instances. The accumulation is what changes what the betrayed partner's body expects.

This is why grand gestures do so little. A vacation, a gift, a long letter — none of them generate evidence, because none of them are repeatable and none of them were in question. What generates evidence is being where you said you would be at the time you said, volunteering the awkward information before it is asked for, answering the same question the same way in month five as in month one, and coming home in an ordinary mood on an ordinary Tuesday.

The involved partner usually underestimates how long this takes. Twelve to twenty-four months of consistency is a realistic frame for couples doing this work well, and the betrayed partner's nervous system will keep running checks long after their reasoning has been satisfied.

Reframe

Reassurance is a withdrawal from trust. Consistency is a deposit. Right now you are running a deficit, and only time in the black closes it.

4.2

Transparency with an exit

Understand

In the early months, open access to phones, accounts, and calendars usually helps. It lowers the search behavior that keeps the betrayed partner in a state of alarm, and it gives the involved partner a structure in which honesty is easy to demonstrate rather than merely asserted.

The risk is that a temporary measure quietly becomes the architecture. Two years on, some couples are still running a monitoring relationship in which one adult is the auditor and the other is the audited — a role structure that reliably kills desire and eventually breeds a new kind of secrecy, since being watched teaches a person to become better at not being seen.

The way out is to define, now, what the end of it looks like: what has to be true, for how long, before the arrangement is stepped down. Write it as conditions and a date rather than a feeling, because "when I feel safe" has no threshold and cannot be met.

Reframe

The goal is not a partner who can be monitored. It is a partner who volunteers the thing you would never have found.

Act

Transparency Agreement

Tool 4.2

If checking has become compulsive

Hours lost to searching, repeated re-reading of what you already found, or checking that gives ten minutes of relief and then more dread — that is a pattern that tends to intensify rather than resolve with more access. 4.3

The line that isn't straight

Understand

Recovery does not improve steadily. Months three and four are frequently worse than month two — the shock wears off, ordinary life resumes, and grief arrives on a schedule of its own. Anniversaries, songs, a place, a phone buzzing at the wrong moment, a scene in a film: the injury reactivates without permission.

What both partners need at that point is a shared explanation of what is happening, so that a bad Thursday does not get read as a referendum. The betrayed partner's task in the moment is to distinguish present evidence from expectation — my body is telling me it is happening again; what do I actually know right now? — without pretending the reaction is irrational. The involved partner's task is to answer the trigger without visible weariness, for far longer than feels fair, because visible weariness teaches the betrayed partner to stop bringing it up rather than to stop feeling it.

Setbacks are not evidence of failure. A setback that shortens — three days becoming a day, becoming an hour — is exactly what recovery looks like from inside.

Reframe

"Still?" is the most expensive word in year one. The honest answer is yes, still, and asking it makes the next one arrive alone.

Act

When a trigger hits — the betrayed partner

Name it out loud rather than acting from it: "I'm triggered, it's the hotel scene, it isn't an accusation." Then ask for the specific thing that helps — a fact, contact, presence, twenty minutes alone.

When a trigger hits — the involved partner

Don't defend, don't sigh, don't point out how far you've come. Answer the question again. Stay in the room. "I'm here, it's over, ask me anything you need" costs you five minutes and buys a month.

Tool 4.3
4.4

Intimacy at a pace you can both consent to

Understand

Sex after discovery goes in unpredictable directions. Some couples experience an intense, urgent reconnection in the first weeks; some find that touch has become unbearable; many alternate between the two within the same week. All of these are common, and none of them is a reliable indicator of where the relationship is heading.

Two failure modes are worth naming. Using sex as proof — that it's fine, that you're wanted, that the other person is still chosen — loads an act that cannot carry that weight and usually leaves both partners lonelier. And treating physical reconnection as the finish line skips the emotional safety it depends on: for most betrayed partners, being able to be exposed again requires having been listened to first, and repeatedly.

Consent here is ongoing and specific, and it belongs to both partners. Either of you may want to stop at any point, and stopping should be entirely unremarkable — no interrogation about what it means, no scorekeeping the next day.

Reframe

Closeness that has to be performed as evidence isn't closeness. It's an audit with the lights off.

4.5

Measuring repair

Understand

"Do we feel better?" is a poor instrument. Feelings after betrayal move with sleep, hormones, anniversaries, and whatever happened at work — you can have a good week inside a dying marriage and a wretched week inside one that is genuinely healing.

Better questions are behavioral and observable. Is honesty increasing, including about small things where lying would be easier? Is follow-through matching what was promised? Are hard conversations recovering faster? Is the need to verify slowly decreasing — not because checking was forbidden, but because it keeps returning nothing?

Rate these monthly, separately, then compare. The gap between two partners' ratings is usually more informative than either number, and a widening gap over several months is worth taking to a therapist rather than arguing about.

Reframe

Ask not "are we happier?" but "are we becoming more honest, more responsible, and better at conflict than we were?"

5.1

Is there something here to repair

Understand

Repair requires specific conditions, and their absence is information rather than an insult. The affair is genuinely over. Responsibility has been taken without a co-signer. The involved partner can tolerate the betrayed partner's pain without collapsing or turning it back on them. Behavior has actually changed, not just been promised. And both people are willing — not certain, willing — to try.

Willingness is the honest word. Almost nobody is certain at this stage, and waiting for certainty before working is how couples spend a year in limbo. What is required is enough willingness to run an experiment with a defined length and defined terms.

Score this separately. If one of you is at 2 and the other is at 8, that gap is the actual subject of the next conversation — and structured discernment work, which explicitly holds the door open in both directions, is usually a better fit than couples therapy aimed at reconciliation.

Reframe

You do not need to know whether you want to stay married. You need to know whether you are willing to find out on purpose.

5.2

Four things that get confused

Understand

Forgiveness, trust, reconciliation, and staying married are four separate processes, and treating them as one package is the source of an enormous amount of pressure in these months.

Forgiveness is an internal release of the demand that the past be different. It can happen in a marriage that ends, and it can fail to happen in a marriage that continues. It cannot be given on schedule, and a partner who asks for it as proof of progress has misunderstood what it is.

Trust is an assessment of reliability built from evidence. Reconciliation is the joint rebuilding of a relationship. Staying is a decision, sometimes made for reasons — children, faith, finances, a shared life worth more than this — that do not require the other three to be complete. The clarity to have here is which of the four you are each actually being asked for right now.

Reframe

Nobody is owed forgiveness on a timetable. And no one has to have forgiven in order to keep working.

5.3

Three legitimate paths

Understand

There are three honest outcomes, and this course does not favor one. What it favors is that whichever you take, you take it deliberately, with terms you both understand, rather than drifting until circumstances decide.

Values are the most useful compass at this juncture, and they are more stable than feelings. Not "do I feel in love this week" but: what kind of person do I want to have been in this? What do I want my children to have watched me do? What could I live with, and what would I be unable to look at in ten years?

Note that a decision to end can be made well or badly, and that ending well is a genuine skill — particularly where there are children, who will need both parents functional far longer than the marriage lasted.

Reframe

Drift is also a decision. It is just one you make by not making it, in installments, over about two years.

Act

Active rebuilding

Both partners in. Defined work, defined support, a review date. Requires most of the readiness conditions to be present.

Structured discernment

A set period — often six weeks to three months — with agreed terms about living arrangements, contact, and decisions, held open in both directions rather than as a soft exit.

Values-Based Decision Guide

Tool 5.3

A note about pressure

Family, friends, and communities often have strong opinions and deliver them with confidence. They will not live inside the result. If you are being pressed toward either outcome — by anyone, including on religious grounds — it is worth saying out loud, here, whose voice you are hearing when you imagine each path.

5.4

The next ninety days

Understand

A decision that stays a feeling evaporates within about a week. Translate it into commitments specific enough that a neutral observer could tell, ninety days from now, whether they happened.

Include individual work, not only couple work. Whatever you decide about the marriage, each of you took patterns into it that will follow you out of it. Include the ordinary infrastructure too — how often you talk, who has the children when, what happens with money — because these are what the next three months will actually consist of.

Then set the reassessment date and put it on a calendar. On that day you are not asking whether it feels resolved. You are asking whether the commitments happened, whether the scorecard moved, and what the evidence now supports.

Reframe

Ninety days from now, the question is not "do we feel better." It is "what actually happened, and what does it show."

Act

Ninety-Day Plan

Tool 5.4

Define progress before you measure it

At the reassessment, ask these four rather than "are we okay?" Are we more honest than we were three months ago? Is responsibility still being held without argument? Are we emotionally accessible to each other more often than not? Do conflicts end better than they used to? Answer them with examples, not impressions.

Whatever you decide, decide it with your eyes open — and be able to say, later, exactly why.
Appendix A

Where this comes from

This course is an integration, not an invention. Each module borrows from a body of clinical work that has been tested on couples in exactly this position. If something in here helps, the sources below are where to read further.

Gottman Method

Supplies the physiology and the observable markers: diffuse physiological arousal and the twenty-minute break, softened start-up, repair attempts, negative sentiment override, sliding-door moments, and the insistence that trust is measured in behavior rather than declared. Modules 1, 3, and 4 lean on it most.

Relational Life Therapy — Terry Real

Supplies the stance on accountability: harm is held whole by the person who did it, context never functions as a co-signer, and grandiosity and shame are treated as two faces of the same collapse. Module 2 is built on this.

Emotionally Focused Therapy — Sue Johnson

Supplies the cycle — pursue and withdraw as a loop rather than a fault — and the listener's work of reaching past content for the attachment fear underneath. It also frames betrayal as an injury to the bond itself, which is why reassurance fails and re-engagement works. Modules 2, 3, and 4.

Bowen Family Systems

Supplies differentiation, triangles, and the family-of-origin lens: what each partner learned about honesty, conflict, and unhappiness long before they met. Module 2.

Satir's Experiential Model

Supplies the survival stances — placating, blaming, super-reasonable, irrelevant — and the alternative of leveling: saying the true thing without attack or collapse. Modules 2 and 3.

Milan Systemic Practice

Supplies circularity: in a repeating loop there is no first move to locate, and asking who started it produces argument rather than information. Used carefully in Module 2, where it must never be allowed to dilute individual responsibility for deception.

Narrative Therapy — White & Epston

Supplies the shared account: externalizing the problem, thickening a story reduced to its worst chapter, and building a version of events both partners can carry. Module 2.

Bronfenbrenner's Ecological Model

Supplies the layers around the couple — children, extended family, workplace, congregation, community — and the reminder that disclosure decisions reshape systems that outlast the crisis. Modules 1 and 5.

Discernment counseling

Supplies the third path in Module 5: a short, structured process for mixed-agenda couples that deliberately holds both outcomes open rather than presuming reconciliation.

Appendix B

For the clinician

A short companion for therapists assigning this course between sessions, or using it as the spine of a structured couples protocol.

Sequencing

The modules are ordered for a reason and do not survive being reshuffled. Understanding (Module 2) before stabilization (Module 1) invites the relational-context conversation while the betrayed partner is still flooded, and it reliably lands as blame. Trust-building (Module 4) before an ownership statement exists produces behavioral compliance without meaning. Decision work (Module 5) attempted in the first weeks is panic wearing the clothing of resolve.

A workable pace is two to four weeks per module, with Module 1 open-ended. Assign one lesson between sessions and use the tool as the session's material rather than reviewing it as homework.

Contraindications and cautions

Conjoint work of this kind is contraindicated where there is intimate partner violence or coercive control, where safety planning is the priority, or where an untreated addiction or acute psychiatric presentation makes the containment this course assumes unachievable. Ongoing, undisclosed contact with the affair partner is also a contraindication for Modules 2 through 5 — the material presumes containment has been established, and proceeding without it teaches the betrayed partner that the process itself cannot be trusted.

Hold a clear position on secrets before beginning, and state it to both partners. Working with a couple while holding one partner's undisclosed ongoing affair places the therapist inside the deception.

What to watch for

  • Premature forgiveness pressure — often from family, a faith community, or the involved partner's shame — presented as spiritual or emotional maturity.
  • The shame-collapse pattern in 2.4: every account of the injury reorganizing around consoling the person who caused it.
  • Detail-seeking that escalates rather than settles, and checking behavior that has become compulsive (4.2). Both usually need individual work alongside the couple work.
  • A widening gap between partners' scorecard ratings across months (4.5) — more informative than either partner's number.
  • Mixed agendas presented as reconciliation. Where readiness scores diverge sharply, discernment is the more honest frame.
  • Trauma symptoms in the betrayed partner — intrusive imagery, hypervigilance, sleep disruption persisting past the acute period — warranting individual referral and, where indicated, trauma-focused treatment.

On formal disclosure

Module 2 builds a shared narrative, not a full therapeutic disclosure. Where a structured disclosure with a written account and a prepared impact statement is indicated — commonly where there is a long history of concealment or compulsive sexual behavior — it should be prepared and facilitated by a trained clinician, with each partner supported individually, rather than attempted from a worksheet at the kitchen table.

Using the tools

Every writing space here is designed to be completed separately by each partner and compared in session; the divergence is the clinical material. The page saves entries in the couple's own browser and transmits nothing. Printing produces a clean workbook if you would rather hand them paper — the print layout drops the navigation, timers, and buttons.
