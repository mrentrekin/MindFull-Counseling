A private executive function reflection

It isn't one thing.
It's nine.

"I can't focus," "I'm lazy," "I have ADHD" — the words people reach for are blunt instruments for something with parts. Executive function is a set of separate skills, and most people are steady in several and struggling in two or three. This reflection takes them apart one at a time, tells you which are actually giving you trouble, and gives you strategies built for that specific function rather than general advice about willpower.

The premise

Executive function is not effort. It's the machinery that turns intention into action — and machinery is repairable in parts.

The nine functions here are drawn from the executive function literature used in clinical and educational practice — Barkley's work on inhibition and self-regulation, Diamond's core functions, and the practical taxonomy Dawson and Guare built for coaching.

What this is, and what it can't do

This is not an ADHD test. It cannot tell you whether you have ADHD, or any other condition, and it isn't scored against a clinical norm. What it does is separate nine skills that usually get discussed as one lump, so you can see which ones are actually costing you something.

That distinction matters practically, because the same difficulties are produced by many different things: sleep debt, anxiety, depression, grief, chronic pain, thyroid problems, perimenopause, a head injury, substance use, trauma, autism, ADHD — or a workload no executive function could carry. A pattern here tells you where to look. It does not tell you why.

If what you find is costing you a job, a degree, or a relationship, that's worth a proper evaluation with someone who can look at history, context, and everything else on that list.

Your nine functions, one at a time

Ordered from most friction to least. Read the top three carefully and ignore the rest for now — trying to fix nine things at once is itself an executive function problem.

Start with one

Pick a single strategy from your highest-friction function and run it for one week.

Choose by cost, not by importance: the strategy you'll actually still be doing on a bad Thursday beats the ideal system you'll abandon by Wednesday. Executive function strategies fail almost exclusively because they require executive function to maintain — so the good ones move the work into the environment, where it doesn't depend on how you feel that day.

  • Externalize it. If it has to be remembered, it will be forgotten. Put it in a place that will interrupt you at the moment it matters.
  • Shrink the start. Most stuck tasks are stuck at the first step. Make the first step embarrassingly small and specific.
  • Add a body. Working alongside another person — in the room or on a video call — reliably does what self-discipline does not.
  • Design for the bad day. A system that only works when you're rested isn't a system.

What this reflection cannot tell you

It cannot tell you whether you have ADHD. It's not a screener, it isn't normed against a clinical population, and self-report on a good day looks different from self-report on a bad one. What it gives you is vocabulary and a starting place.

Everything measured here is produced by many different causes. Sleep debt, anxiety, depression, grief, chronic pain, thyroid dysfunction, perimenopause, concussion, medication side effects, substance use, trauma, autism, ADHD, and an unmanageable workload all show up in these nine functions, often identically. Several can be true at once. Treating the wrong one is why people conclude that nothing works.

Two things worth checking before anything else, because they're common and reversible: how much you're actually sleeping, and whether the demands currently on you would overwhelm anyone.

Worth a professional evaluation if what you scored is affecting work, school, parenting, health, or a relationship; if it has been true since childhood; if it got noticeably worse at an identifiable point; or if low mood, anxiety, or hopelessness travels with it. Bring this page — a clinician can do more with "initiation and time management, most days, for years" than with "I can't focus."

If you take this to someone

What actually helps a clinician, in rough order of usefulness:

  • Which functions, and how often — not a general sense of struggling.
  • When it started, and whether there was a point it got worse.
  • What it has cost: the missed deadline, the late fee, the argument, the job.
  • What you've already tried, and what specifically happened when it stopped working.
  • Sleep, alcohol, medications, and anything medical currently in play.
  • Whether anyone in your family has a similar history.

An evaluation for adults typically involves a clinical interview about your history, standardized rating scales, and sometimes input from someone who knew you as a child. It is not a single computer test, and being organized enough to arrive on time doesn't disqualify anyone from anything.
